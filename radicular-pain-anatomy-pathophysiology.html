Radicular Pain: Anatomy & Pathophysiology

Radicular pain is best understood by connecting three pieces of anatomy:

  • The intervertebral disc
  • The neural foramen and lateral recess
  • The spinal nerve roots

The intervertebral disc

Intervertebral discs are fibrocartilaginous structures that sit between adjacent vertebral bodies. Their major functions include:

  • Absorbing compressive forces
  • Distributing load across the spine
  • Allowing controlled movement between adjacent vertebrae
  • Helping maintain disc height and foraminal space

Each intervertebral disc is composed of two main parts:

  • The nucleus pulposus is the gelatinous central portion that is rich in water and proteoglycans, which helps support its major function of absorbing compressive forces and distributing load.
  • The annulus fibrosus consists of fibrocartilaginous outer rings that are composed of concentric lamellae of collagen fibers, enabling resistance to tension, shearing, and rotational forces.

The interface between an intervertebral disc and an adjacent vertebral body is referred to as the cartilaginous endplate. These interfaces allow the diffusion of nutrients and waste, which is important since discs in adults are largely avascular, and thus have limited healing potential.

With degeneration, discs lose water and proteoglycan content, reducing their ability to distribute load and making them more vulnerable to fissuring, bulging, or herniation.

Term Meaning
Annular fissure A tear or separation within the annulus fibrosus
Disc bulge Broad-based extension of disc tissue beyond the normal disc margin
Disc herniation Focal displacement of disc material beyond the normal disc margin
Disc extrusion Herniated disc material extends outward with a narrower connection to the parent disc
Disc sequestration Herniated disc material separates completely from the parent disc

Disc herniation and nerve root irritation

A disc herniation can produce radicular pain through both mechanical compression and chemical inflammation.

Mechanical compression occurs when disc material physically narrows the space around a nerve root. Chemical irritation occurs because disc material and local inflammatory mediators can irritate the nerve root even when compression is not severe.

This helps explain why the degree of pain does not always perfectly match the size of the herniation seen on imaging.

NoteKey idea

Radicular pain is not always just a “pinched nerve.” It can involve both nerve root compression and nerve root inflammation.

Traversing versus exiting nerve roots

The concept of traversing versus exiting nerve roots can be tricky to understand at first. However, this concept is important because it helps explain why the level of a disc herniation does not always match the level of the affected nerve root.

In the lumbar spine, each nerve root exits the spinal canal under the vertebra with the same number. For example, the L4 nerve root exits under the L4 vertebra through the L4-L5 neural foramen. Similarly, the L5 nerve root exits under the L5 vertebra through the L5-S1 neural foramen.

These are referred to as the exiting nerve roots at their respective levels.

Now let’s take the L4-L5 disc level as an example. At this level, the L4 vertebra is above, the L4-L5 disc is in the middle, and the L5 vertebra is below. The L4 nerve root is the exiting nerve root because it leaves the spinal canal through the L4-L5 neural foramen. The L5 nerve root, however, does not exit at this level. Instead, it continues traveling downward through the lateral recess before eventually exiting at the L5-S1 foramen. This makes the L5 nerve root the traversing nerve root at the L4-L5 disc level.

This distinction matters because the presentation of a herniated disc depends on where exactly the disc material herniates.

  • A paracentral disc herniation is one that herniates just lateral to the midline into the lateral recess. At the L4-L5 level, this can affect the traversing L5 nerve root, causing compression or inflammation of the L5 root and symptoms that follow an L5 distribution.
  • A foraminal disc herniation extends into the intervertebral foramen, where the exiting nerve root leaves the spinal canal. At the L4-L5 level, this can affect the exiting L4 nerve root, causing symptoms that follow an L4 distribution.
  • An extraforaminal or far lateral disc herniation extends even farther laterally, beyond the neural foramen. Like a foraminal herniation, this most often affects the exiting nerve root. At L4-L5, this would usually involve the exiting L4 nerve root.

In some cases, a disc herniation may be central. Central disc herniations can be dangerous if they are large enough because they may compress multiple nerve roots within the spinal canal. Severe central compression can contribute to cauda equina syndrome, which requires urgent assessment, as we will discuss later.

Herniation location Root usually affected Example at L4-L5
Paracentral / lateral recess Traversing nerve root L5 root
Foraminal / far lateral Exiting nerve root L4 root
Central May affect multiple roots if large Cauda equina risk if severe

At the L4-L5 disc, a paracentral herniation usually affects the traversing L5 nerve root, while a foraminal or far lateral herniation more often affects the exiting L4 nerve root.

This distinction is high-yield because the disc level and the affected nerve root are not always the same.

Radicular pain and neuropathic pain

Radicular pain often has neuropathic features because the pain is generated by irritation of a nerve root. Patients may describe symptoms as shooting, burning, electric, sharp, or radiating.

If the nerve root is irritated but still functioning normally, the patient may have radicular pain without objective neurologic deficits. If the nerve root is impaired, the patient may develop radiculopathy with weakness, sensory loss, or reflex change.

Radicular pain versus radiculopathy

Radicular pain is closely related to, but not identical to, radiculopathy.

Term Meaning
Radicular pain Nerve-root-mediated radiating pain
Radiculopathy Objective nerve root dysfunction, such as weakness, sensory loss, or reflex change

A patient can have radicular pain without clear objective neurologic deficits. Conversely, radiculopathy implies that the nerve root is not only irritated, but also functionally impaired.

Common causes

The classic teaching example is radicular pain from a lumbar disc herniation. This is especially common in younger or middle-aged patients.

However, radicular pain can also occur from degenerative narrowing, such as foraminal stenosis or lateral recess stenosis, which becomes more common with age.
