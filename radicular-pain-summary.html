Lumbar Radicular Pain Summary

High-yield summary

Radicular pain is nerve-root-mediated radiating pain. In the lumbar spine, it usually presents as leg-dominant pain radiating from the low back or buttock into the lower limb.

Radicular pain is related to, but not identical to, radiculopathy:

  • Radicular pain: radiating nerve-root-mediated pain
  • Radiculopathy: objective nerve root dysfunction, such as weakness, sensory loss, or reflex change

Key causes

In acute lumbar radicular pain, disc herniation is the most common cause, particularly in younger and middle-aged adults. Herniated disc material can compress and chemically inflame a nerve root.

By contrast, chronic cases in older adults are more often caused by degenerative narrowing of the lateral recess or neural foramen due to disc height loss, facet hypertrophy or osteophytes, degenerative spondylolisthesis, or a synovial cyst.

Less common but serious causes include:

  • Tumour
  • Infection
  • Fracture or significant trauma
  • Inflammatory disease

Key anatomy

The most common disc-herniation level is L4-L5, followed by L5-S1. These lower lumbar levels experience high mechanical load and substantial movement.

The distinction between exiting and traversing nerve roots matters because the direction of a disc herniation helps determine which nerve root is affected. More lateral herniations can involve the neural foramen and affect the exiting nerve root, while more central or paracentral herniations can involve the lateral recess and affect the traversing nerve root.

The table below summarizes the main herniation zones and the nerve roots they are most likely to affect:

Herniation zone Description
Central Disc material herniates toward the middle of the spinal canal and may affect multiple nerve roots if the herniation is large.
Paracentral Disc material herniates just lateral to the midline into the lateral recess and usually affects the traversing nerve root; at L4-L5, this is the L5 nerve root.
Foraminal Disc material extends into the neural foramen and usually affects the exiting nerve root; at L4-L5, this is the L4 nerve root.
Extraforaminal Disc material extends beyond the neural foramen and usually affects the exiting nerve root; at L4-L5, this is the L4 nerve root.

Key clinical clues

The most useful clinical clues are:

  • Leg-dominant pain
  • Neuropathic quality, such as shooting, burning, electric, or sharp pain
  • Dermatomal or nerve-root pattern
  • Objective neurologic findings
  • Positive neural tension signs

Test How it is performed Suggests
Straight leg raise (Lasègue test) With the patient lying supine, passively raise the straightened leg to about 30–70° and assess whether this reproduces radiating leg pain Lower lumbar radicular irritation, especially L5/S1

Investigations

Most acute radicular pain does not require immediate imaging if there are no red flags or severe neurologic deficits.

MRI is the preferred imaging test when imaging is needed. However, imaging must match the clinical picture because disc bulges and degenerative findings are common in asymptomatic people.

EMG/NCS can help when the diagnosis is unclear, symptoms are chronic, or imaging does not match the clinical picture.

Management

Initial management is usually conservative unless red flags or progressive neurologic deficits are present.

Most patients improve with conservative management:

  • Education and reassurance
  • Staying active as tolerated
  • Analgesics or NSAIDs when appropriate
  • Selected neuropathic pain medications
  • Physiotherapy and graded return to function

Some patients with severe, persistent, or function-limiting leg-dominant symptoms and concordant imaging may benefit from an epidural steroid injection.

Rare cases require urgent specialist assessment or surgical referral, particularly when there is progressive neurologic deficit, concern for cauda equina syndrome, severe refractory pain, or persistent function-limiting symptoms with matching imaging.

Final takeaway

Radicular pain is usually caused by disc herniation or degenerative narrowing affecting a spinal nerve root. The pattern depends on the nerve root involved and whether the exiting or traversing root is affected.

The clinical priority is to identify red flags, localize the likely nerve root, and match symptoms to imaging when needed. Choose management based on the overall picture, with a particular focus on improving function.
