Radicular Pain: Clinical Localization

Radicular pain is not only about recognizing that pain travels down the leg. Clinically, the goal is to decide whether the pattern fits a spinal nerve root, which nerve root is most likely involved, and whether there is evidence of objective nerve root dysfunction.

This section focuses on the clinical anatomy needed to localize radicular pain and distinguish radicular pain from radiculopathy.

Nerve root anatomy

Each spinal nerve root carries sensory and motor information.

  • The dorsal root carries sensory information toward the spinal cord.
  • The ventral root carries motor information away from the spinal cord.
  • The dorsal root ganglion contains the cell bodies of sensory neurons.
  • After the dorsal and ventral roots join, they form a mixed spinal nerve.
  • The spinal nerve then divides into dorsal and ventral rami.

The ventral rami contribute to major nerve plexuses, such as the brachial plexus in the upper limb and the lumbosacral plexus in the lower limb.

This matters clinically because a spinal nerve root problem can sometimes resemble a peripheral nerve problem. Localization depends on the pattern of pain, sensory symptoms, weakness, reflex changes, and imaging findings.

NoteKey idea

A nerve root problem should produce a pattern that makes anatomic sense. The pattern may involve pain, sensation, strength, and reflexes.

Dermatomes

A dermatome is an area of skin supplied mainly by one spinal nerve root.

In radicular pain, the pain or sensory symptoms may follow a dermatomal pattern. For example, irritation of the L5 nerve root often causes symptoms that travel down the lateral leg toward the dorsum of the foot.

Dermatomes are clinically useful, but they are not perfect. Patients do not always describe symptoms in a clean textbook distribution, and dermatomes can overlap. This is why dermatomes should be interpreted alongside the rest of the history, neurologic examination, and imaging.

Nerve root Common sensory distribution
L3 Anterior thigh
L4 Medial leg / medial ankle
L5 Lateral leg / dorsum of foot / great toe
S1 Posterior leg / lateral foot / sole

Myotomes

A myotome is a group of muscles supplied mainly by one spinal nerve root.

Myotomes help identify whether radicular pain has progressed to radiculopathy, meaning objective nerve root dysfunction. Weakness in a myotomal pattern suggests that the affected nerve root is not only irritated, but also functionally impaired.

Nerve root Common motor finding
L3 Weak knee extension
L4 Weak knee extension or ankle dorsiflexion
L5 Weak great toe extension or ankle dorsiflexion
S1 Weak plantarflexion

For example, a patient with radiating lateral leg pain and weakness of great toe extension may have an L5 radiculopathy.

Reflexes

Reflex testing can provide another clue to nerve root involvement.

Nerve root Reflex
L4 Patellar reflex
S1 Achilles reflex

A reduced reflex is not required for radiculopathy, but when present, it can help localize the affected root. For example, radiating posterior leg pain with a reduced Achilles reflex would support S1 involvement.

Referral patterns

Radicular pain is often described as radiating, shooting, burning, electric, sharp, or lancinating. These descriptors suggest neuropathic-type pain, but they are not enough on their own.

The key question is whether the pain follows a plausible nerve root pattern.

For example:

  • Pain radiating from the low back or buttock into the lateral leg and dorsum of the foot suggests possible L5 involvement.
  • Pain radiating into the posterior leg and lateral foot suggests possible S1 involvement.
  • Pain that stays only in the low back without leg radiation is less suggestive of radicular pain.

Radicular pain can also occur in the cervical spine. In that setting, patients may describe neck pain radiating into the shoulder, arm, forearm, or hand depending on the affected cervical nerve root.

Radicular pain versus neuropathic pain

Radicular pain often has neuropathic features because the pain is generated by irritation of a nerve root. Patients may describe the pain as:

  • Shooting
  • Burning
  • Electric
  • Sharp
  • Lancinating
  • Radiating

However, neuropathic pain is a broader category. It can arise from injury or disease affecting nerves, nerve roots, the spinal cord, or the brain. Radicular pain is more specific: it refers to pain generated by irritation of a spinal nerve root.

In other words, radicular pain is often neuropathic in quality, but not all neuropathic pain is radicular.

Radicular pain versus radiculopathy

Radicular pain is closely related to, but not identical to, radiculopathy.

Term Meaning
Radicular pain Nerve-root-mediated radiating pain
Radiculopathy Objective nerve root dysfunction, such as weakness, sensory loss, or reflex change

A patient can have radicular pain without objective neurologic deficits. In this situation, the nerve root may be irritated or inflamed, but still functioning normally.

Radiculopathy implies that the nerve root is not only irritated, but also functionally impaired. This may occur with significant compression, inflammation, or recurrent/prolonged irritation of the nerve root. Clinically, this can present with objective motor weakness, dermatomal sensory loss, or reduced reflexes.

A patient has sharp pain radiating from the low back down the lateral leg to the dorsum of the foot, but strength, sensation, and reflexes are normal. Is this radicular pain or radiculopathy?

The pain pattern suggests irritation of a spinal nerve root, likely L5. However, radiculopathy requires objective nerve root dysfunction, such as weakness, sensory loss, or reflex change.

Common causes of radicular pain

Radicular pain can occur whenever a spinal nerve root is mechanically compressed, chemically irritated, or inflamed.

The most common causes are disc herniation and degenerative narrowing. Disc herniation is a classic cause in younger or middle-aged patients, while degenerative stenosis becomes more common with age.

Cause Mechanism
Disc herniation Disc material compresses or inflames a nerve root
Foraminal stenosis Narrowing of the neural foramen compresses the exiting nerve root
Lateral recess stenosis Narrowing of the lateral recess compresses the traversing nerve root
Degenerative spondylolisthesis Vertebral slippage narrows the canal, recess, or foramen
Facet hypertrophy / osteophytes Degenerative bony enlargement contributes to nerve root compression
Synovial cyst Facet-related cyst compresses nearby neural structures
Less common serious causes Tumour, infection, trauma, or inflammatory disease

Less common causes should be considered when the presentation is atypical or red flags are present. These include infection, malignancy, fracture or trauma, inflammatory disease, post-surgical scarring, and cystic lesions or masses.

NoteKey idea

Disc herniation and degenerative narrowing are the common causes, but atypical features or red flags should prompt consideration of less common but important causes.
